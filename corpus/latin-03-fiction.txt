The lighthouse keeper had not seen another human being in eleven weeks, and he had stopped counting the days by anything other than the tide. Every morning he climbed the hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room, cleaned the lens with a cloth kept soft by years of the same motion, and looked out at a horizon that never once offered him a ship worth noting in the log.

He had taken the posting after his wife died, not because he wanted solitude exactly, but because the alternative, staying in the house they had shared, felt like a slower and less honest way of disappearing. Out here the disappearing was at least visible. He could watch himself grow quieter, could measure it against the silence of the rocks and the gulls, and there was a strange comfort in that, the way there is comfort in any process you can actually observe.

On the seventy-ninth day a boat did come, small and low in the water, and the man who climbed out onto the landing introduced himself as an inspector sent to check the light's maintenance records. The keeper found that he had almost forgotten how to shape words for another person. He answered in short sentences, surprised at how much effort it took, and when the inspector left three hours later, promising to send the replacement parts for the foghorn within the month, the keeper stood on the landing until the boat was a shape and then a memory.

That night he wrote in the log, for the first time in weeks, more than the weather and the tide. Visitor today, he wrote. Strange to hear my own voice.
