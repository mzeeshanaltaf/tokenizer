The James Webb Space Telescope has fundamentally changed how astronomers study the early universe. Unlike its predecessor, Hubble, which observes primarily in visible and ultraviolet light, Webb was designed to see in the infrared. This distinction matters enormously for cosmology, because light from the most distant galaxies has been stretched by the expansion of space itself, shifting it out of the visible spectrum and into wavelengths that only an infrared observatory can detect.

Since its first images were released, Webb has discovered galaxies that appear to be more massive and more structured than models of early galaxy formation predicted. Some of these galaxies appear to have formed within the first few hundred million years after the Big Bang, a timeline that has forced theorists to reconsider how quickly gas collapses into stars in the young universe. A handful of candidates, still awaiting spectroscopic confirmation, would push that timeline back even further if verified.

Webb's instruments also allow astronomers to study the atmospheres of exoplanets in unprecedented detail. By observing a planet as it passes in front of its host star, researchers can measure how starlight is absorbed by molecules in the planet's atmosphere, revealing the presence of water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, and other compounds. This technique, called transmission spectroscopy, has already identified atmospheric signatures on several worlds that were previously little more than a mass and a radius in a catalog.

None of this would be possible without the telescope's location roughly 1.5 million kilometers from Earth, at the second Lagrange point, where the combined gravity of the Sun and Earth allows a spacecraft to maintain a stable orbit while staying in the deep, cold shadow the instruments require to function. Getting there, and unfolding a mirror larger than any that had flown before, remains one of the more delicate engineering feats of the last decade.
