The construction of the Suez Canal, completed in 1869, reshaped global trade in ways that are easy to underestimate today. Before the canal opened, a ship sailing from London to Bombay had to round the Cape of Good Hope, a journey of roughly three months. The canal cut that voyage down to a matter of weeks, and shipping companies reorganized entire fleets around the new route almost immediately.

The project was led by Ferdinand de Lesseps, a French diplomat with no formal engineering training, who spent more than a decade raising funds, negotiating with the Ottoman authorities who controlled Egypt, and defending the canal's feasibility against skeptical engineers who doubted a sea-level waterway could be dug across a hundred miles of desert without locks. Tens of thousands of Egyptian laborers were conscripted to dig the canal by hand in its early years, under conditions that later inquiries described as closer to forced labor than paid employment, before steam-powered dredgers took over the bulk of the excavation.

Britain, which had initially declined to invest, later purchased a controlling share of the Suez Canal Company in 1875 when the Egyptian ruler Isma'il Pasha, deep in debt, sold his shares to the British government. That purchase gave Britain enormous strategic leverage over the route to India, and the canal's importance to imperial trade was a major factor behind Britain's occupation of Egypt in 1882, an occupation that would last, in one form or another, for the next seventy years.

The canal's later history includes its nationalization by Gamal Abdel Nasser in 1956, which triggered a brief war involving Britain, France, and Israel, and cemented the canal's status as one of the most politically sensitive strips of water on the planet.
